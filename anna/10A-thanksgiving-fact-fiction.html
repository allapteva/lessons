                    Food & history

                    Five Thanksgiving stories: true or not?

                    Thanksgiving has been a national holiday in the USA since 1863, when Abraham Lincoln made it one after a long campaign by the writer Sarah Josepha Hale. Tomorrow more than forty million turkeys will be eaten, and with every turkey come the stories. But how many of them are true?

                    “Turkey makes you sleepy.” Turkey contains tryptophan, a chemical that the brain uses to make the sleep hormone melatonin. True, but chicken and beef contain about the same amount. The idea that the turkey alone sends you to sleep is far-fetched. A huge meal, the potatoes and the pies, a glass of wine and a long day in the kitchen are a much more plausible explanation.

                    “The Pilgrims ate turkey at the first Thanksgiving.” In the autumn of 1621 about fifty English settlers and some ninety Wampanoag men shared a feast that lasted three days. One settler wrote that the Wampanoag brought five deer and that four men went out to shoot birds. They may have come back with wild turkeys, which were common that year, so the story is not totally implausible. But it is only speculative: nobody wrote the word “turkey” down.

                    “There was pumpkin pie on the table.” The settlers had pumpkins, but they probably had no ovens and very little butter or wheat flour for pastry. Most historians think the pie is pure fiction: if there was pumpkin, it was probably cooked in the fire.

                    “Benjamin Franklin wanted the turkey as the national bird.” Half true. In a letter to his daughter in 1784, Franklin called the bald eagle “a bird of bad moral character” and the turkey “a much more respectable bird”. But he never suggested it to anyone officially: it was a private joke, not a plan.

                    “One day, the turkey will come from a laboratory.” This one is about the future. Meat grown from animal cells went on sale in the USA in 2023, as chicken in a couple of restaurants. A lab-grown turkey on every table faces extreme obstacles: it is still astronomically expensive, so it is quite a way off. But some scientists believe a cheaper product might be achievable in the next twenty years.

                      Grammar

                      All, every, both, neither

                      Small words that say how much or how many. Read the rules, then choose. Then make the sentences true.

                      0 / 0

                      all, every, most

                      • all + plural or uncountable noun: All the guests were hungry.
                      • every + singular noun: Every guest brought something. Every year.
                      • Not all alone as a subject: use everything / everybody. Everything is ready.
                      • most / all / none of + the, my, them: Most of my friends, but Most people.

                      no, none, any · both, neither, either

                      • no + noun, with a positive verb: There’s no gravy. none alone or with of: None of them came.
                      • any = it doesn’t matter which: Come any time.
                      • both (… and), neither (… nor), either (… or): Neither the turkey nor the pie was ready.

                        Make it true

                        Each sentence is false. Say a true sentence with all, every, most, no, none, both, neither…

                        1. All Americans eat turkey at Thanksgiving.
                        2. Both the USA and Canada celebrate Thanksgiving in November.
                        3. None of the guests at the 1621 feast were Native Americans.
                        4. Every pie at a Thanksgiving dinner is a pumpkin pie.
                        5. Neither turkey nor chicken contains tryptophan.
