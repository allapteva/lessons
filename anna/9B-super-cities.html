      Cities & architecture

      Six ideas for a beautiful city

      Why do we fall in love with some cities and hurry to leave others? The writer Alain de Botton believes the answer is not a mystery. He suggests six ideas.

      Order and variety. We like streets that are well organised, with buildings of a similar style, but not so similar that they become boring. Too much order is dull; too much variety is chaos.

      Visible life. A city feels alive when you can see people: in cafés and shops on the ground floor, on benches, walking in the street. Long walls without windows make a street feel dead.

      Compactness. Beautiful cities have clear edges. Instead of spreading out into endless suburbs, they are dense, with squares where people meet.

      Orientation and mystery. A good city is easy to find your way around, thanks to wide main streets, but it also has small, winding streets where you can get a little lost.

      Scale. Buildings should be about five floors high. Very tall towers make people feel small and unimportant.

      Local colour. A city needs its own personality: buildings made of local materials, in a style that suits the climate and the history of the place, so that you always know where you are.

            Furniture, news and the police

            Some nouns can’t be counted in English, some are always plural, and some groups of people can take a plural verb. Read the rules, then choose.

            0 / 0

            Uncountable nouns

            • No a / an, no plural, a singular verb: advice, accommodation, furniture, information, luggage, news, progress, traffic, weather, work. The news is good.
            • For one thing, use a piece of: a piece of advice, a piece of furniture, a piece of news.
            • Some nouns change meaning: paper (the material) / a paper (a newspaper); light / a light (a lamp); time / a time (an occasion).

            Plural and group nouns

            • Always plural: belongings, clothes, outskirts, surroundings, stairs. The outskirts are quiet.
            • Things with two parts: trousers, scissors, glasses; one of them is a pair of: a pair of scissors, never a scissors.
            • Groups of people (staff, team, family, crew) can take a singular or a plural verb. The police always takes a plural verb: The police are coming.
