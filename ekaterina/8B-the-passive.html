The passive, said clearly

We use the passive when what happened matters more than who did it: in history, in the news and in medicine. Today you practise every form of it, and you practise saying whole passive sentences so that each one is easy to understand.

        The passive in every tense

        The recipe never changes: a form of be + the past participle. Only be changes with the tense. Tap the speaker and repeat.

        Tensebe + past participleExample
        Present simpleam / is / are + doneThousands of scans are done every day.
        Present continuousis / are being + doneA new hospital is being built near the station.
        Past simplewas / were + doneThe city was captured in 1812.
        Past continuouswas / were being + doneThe patient was being examined when I arrived.
        Present perfecthas / have been + doneThe results have been sent to your doctor.
        Past perfecthad been + doneThe bridge had been destroyed before the army arrived.
        Futurewill be + doneYou will be told the results tomorrow.
        After a modal or to(to) be + doneChildren must be vaccinated. He needs to be seen by a doctor.

        When do we use it?

        • When we don’t know or don’t need to say who did it: My car was stolen last night.
        • When the action or the result is the important thing: Penicillin was discovered in 1928.
        • In formal and scientific writing: The patients were divided into two groups.

        Three things to remember

        • Say who did it with by, only if it matters: It was built by an engineer.
        • A person can be the subject: They gave me a leaflet. → I was given a leaflet.
        • We say I was born in 1999, never I born.

            Say it clearly

            A sentence is clear when the important words are strong and the small grammar words are weak. In a passive sentence, the strong words are the noun and the past participle, not was, been or by.

            0 / 0

            Weak words

            In the middle of a sentence these words are short and quiet.

            • was /wəz/
            • were /wə/
            • are /ə/
            • been /bɪn/
            • be /bi/
            • has /əz/
            • have /əv/
            • to /tə/
            • a /ə/
            • the /ðə/

            Listen and repeat

            • The city was captured.
            • The soldiers were sent home.
            • The results have been checked.
            • It can’t be done today.

              A short history of ultrasound

              How a tool of war became a window into the body

              Ultrasound is sound that is too high for people to hear. During the First World War it was developed for a military purpose: finding enemy submarines under the sea. Short signals were sent through the water, and when they hit something, an echo came back. This technology is known as sonar.

              After the war, the same idea was used in industry. In shipyards and factories, metal was tested with ultrasound to find cracks that couldn’t be seen from the outside. Then, in 1942, ultrasound was tried in medicine for the first time by an Austrian doctor, Karl Dussik, who hoped to find brain tumours with it.

              The real breakthrough came in Scotland. Ian Donald, a doctor in Glasgow, had learned about radar and sonar when he served in the air force during the Second World War. In the 1950s he borrowed an industrial machine from a local factory and worked with a young engineer, Tom Brown. Together they built a scanner for patients. One woman had been told that she had cancer and that nothing could be done. With the new scanner, a large cyst was found instead. It was removed, and she recovered.

              Their results were published in 1958. At first, the unclear grey pictures were not taken seriously by many doctors. Today ultrasound is used in almost every hospital in the world. No radiation is needed, so it is safe for babies and children, and millions of scans are done every year.

                  Medical English: at the scan

                  You are explaining an ultrasound scan to the mother of a five-year-old boy. Doctors use the passive a lot here, because the procedure is more important than who does it.

                  Listen to a line, then say it. A dot marks a strong word; / marks a short pause. Then read the whole script without stopping.

                  1. Hello. / Your son is going to have an ultrasound scan / of his tummy.
                  2. It doesn’t hurt, / and no radiation is used.
                  3. Some warm gel / will be put on his skin.
                  4. Then a small probe / is moved slowly over his tummy.
                  5. The pictures are shown on this screen, / so you can watch.
                  6. He needs to be kept still / for a few minutes.
                  7. The images will be checked today, / and you’ll be given the results / before you leave.
                  8. Do you have any questions?

                  Role-play

                  Your teacher is the parent, and she is nervous. Explain the scan in your own words, then answer her questions: Will it hurt? Is it safe? How long will it take? When will we be told the results?
