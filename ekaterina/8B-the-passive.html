The passive, said clearly

Castles, cathedrals, battles, and how a tool of war ended up in every hospital. When we talk about history, what happened matters more than who did it, so we need the passive. Today you practise every form of it, the words you need for battles and buildings, and saying them clearly.

        The passive in every form

        The recipe never changes: a form of be + the past participle. Only be changes. Tap the speaker and repeat, copying the rhythm.

        Formbe + past participleExample
        Present simpleam / is / are + doneThe museum is visited by thousands of tourists every summer.
        Present continuousis / are being + doneThe old theatre is being restored at the moment.
        Present perfecthas / have been + doneThe stolen painting has never been found.
        Past simplewas / were + doneThe city was founded in 1703.
        Past continuouswas / were being + doneThe square was closed because a film was being made there.
        Past perfecthad been + doneThe church had been damaged in the war, so it was rebuilt.
        will / be going towill be + done
        is going to be + done        		A new children’s clinic will be opened next year. We are going to be shown round the palace.
        Infinitive with toto be + doneThe bridge had to be closed for repairs.
        Infinitive without tobe + doneTickets can be bought online.
        -ing formbeing + doneNobody likes being told what to do.

        When do we use it?

        • When it isn’t known or important who did it: My bike has been stolen.
        • For processes and in formal writing: The blood is tested in the laboratory.
        • To change the focus. A fire destroyed the church is about the fire. The church was destroyed by a fire is about the church.

        Three things to remember

        • Don’t forget be. Not The house is going to paint but The house is going to be painted.
        • Use by only when the person matters: It was designed by Rastrelli. Most passive sentences have no by.
        • A person can be the subject: They gave us a map. → We were given a map.

                A short history of ultrasound

                How a tool of war became a window into the body

                Ultrasound is sound that is too high for people to hear. During the First World War it was developed for a military purpose: finding enemy submarines under the sea. Short signals were sent through the water, and when they hit something, an echo came back. This technology is known as sonar.

                After the war, the same idea was used in industry. In shipyards and factories, metal was tested with ultrasound to find cracks that couldn’t be seen from the outside. Then, in 1942, ultrasound was tried in medicine for the first time by an Austrian doctor, Karl Dussik, who hoped to find brain tumours with it.

                The real breakthrough came in Scotland. Ian Donald, a doctor in Glasgow, had learned about radar and sonar when he served in the air force during the Second World War. In the 1950s he borrowed an industrial machine from a local factory and worked with a young engineer, Tom Brown. Together they built a scanner for patients. One woman had been told that she had cancer and that nothing could be done. With the new scanner, a large cyst was found instead. It was removed, and she recovered.

                Their results were published in 1958. At first, the unclear grey pictures were not taken seriously by many doctors. Today ultrasound is used in almost every hospital in the world. No radiation is needed, so it is safe for babies and children, and millions of scans are done every year.

                  Medical English: a tour and a scan

                  Two short talks full of passives. First a guide shows visitors round one of the most famous children’s hospitals in the world, Great Ormond Street Hospital in London. Then you explain a scan to a parent.

                  Listen to a line, then say it. A dot marks a strong word; / marks a short pause. Then give the whole tour without stopping.

                  1. Good morning, everyone, / and welcome to Great Ormond Street Hospital.
                  2. The hospital was opened in eighteen fifty-two / by Doctor Charles West.
                  3. It was the first hospital in England / just for children, / and it had only ten beds.
                  4. A lot of money was raised for it / by the writer Charles Dickens.
                  5. In nineteen twenty-nine / the rights to Peter Pan / were given to the hospital / by its author.
                  6. Today / children are sent here / from all over the country.
                  7. New treatments are being developed here / all the time.
                  8. Now please follow me. / You’ll be shown the chapel, / which was built in eighteen seventy-five.

                  Explaining a scan

                  Now you are the doctor. You are explaining an ultrasound scan to the mother of a five-year-old boy. Listen to a line, then say it.

                  1. Hello. / Your son is going to have an ultrasound scan / of his tummy.
                  2. It doesn’t hurt, / and no radiation is used.
                  3. Some warm gel / will be put on his skin.
                  4. Then a small probe / is moved slowly over his tummy.
                  5. The pictures are shown on this screen, / so you can watch.
                  6. He needs to be kept still / for a few minutes.
                  7. The images will be checked today, / and you’ll be given the results / before you leave.
                  8. Do you have any questions?

                  Role-plays

                  1. The scan. Your teacher is the boy’s mother, and she is nervous. Explain the scan in your own words, then answer her questions.

                  2. Your hospital. A doctor from another country is visiting your hospital, and you are the guide. Say when it was built or opened, what is done in each department, what is being repaired or changed now, and what will be opened soon.

                  Speaking

                  1. What is the most interesting historical site you have ever visited? Were you shown round by a guide?
                  2. Think of a famous building in your city. When was it built? Who was it designed by? What is it used for today?
                  3. Is anything in your city being restored or rebuilt at the moment? Has any old building been destroyed that you miss?
                  4. Which battle or war were you taught most about at school? What do you remember?
                  5. Have you seen a historical film or series recently? Which period is it set in? Is it based on real events?
                  6. Would you like to see a battle acted out by people in costumes and armour? Why, or why not?
                  7. Which medical discovery do you think was the most important in history? Who was it made by?
