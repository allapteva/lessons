          Numbers into pictures

          Can you remember a twelve-digit number after looking at it for twenty seconds? Most people can’t: our short-term memory holds only about seven things at a time. Memory champions, who can memorize hundreds of digits in a few minutes, don’t have better brains than the rest of us. They simply use tricks.

          One of the oldest tricks is to turn numbers into pictures, because the brain remembers images far better than symbols. Each digit becomes an object that looks like it: 0 is a ring, 1 is a candle, 2 is a swan, 3 is a bird flying sideways, 4 is a sailing boat, 5 is a fish hook, 6 is a snail, 7 is a boomerang, 8 is a snowman and 9 is a lollipop.

          The second step is to link the pictures into a story. The stranger and funnier the story is, the better. For 5820, for example, imagine a fish hook that catches a snowman, who throws a swan through a ring. Go through the story in your head two or three times, and you will be surprised how easily you can recall it the next day.

          It sounds like a lot of work, but after a week of practice most people find it quick and even fun. And it isn’t only for numbers: the same method can help you remember a shopping list, the points of a presentation, or the names of the people you meet at a party.

                  B. Telling someone what was said

                  present
                  ‘I’m tired.’ → She said (that) she was tired.
                  past, present perfect
                  ‘I’ve lost my keys.’ → He said he had lost his keys.
                  will, can, must
                  ‘I’ll call you.’ → She said she would call me. (can → could, must → had to)
                  yes / no question
                  ‘Are you OK?’ → He asked me if I was OK.
                  wh- question
                  ‘Where do you live?’ → She asked me where I lived.
                  instruction
                  ‘Sit down.’ → He told me to sit down. ‘Don’t move.’ → He told me not to move.
                  request
                  ‘Can you help me?’ → She asked me to help her.
                  say or tell?
                  She said (that)… · She told me (that)… Tell always needs a person.
                  words that change
                  today → that day · tomorrow → the next day · yesterday → the day before · here → there · next week → the following week

                  In questions there is no do / does / did, and the word order is the same as in a normal sentence: She asked me where I lived, not where did I live.
